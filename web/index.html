What is this?

Michael Ruhlman's Ratio claims that the base ingredient ratios determine what kind of baked good you get. This project tests that at scale: 2,231,142 recipes are reduced to five structural parts and scored against the independent class labels Food.com attached to them. The ratios are the features, the tags are the labels, and the two are never allowed to peek at each other.

The result is a partial yes. The classes are described in ratio space, not predicted from it: their centroids sit where the book says they should (cake leans rich, bread leans flour, cookies sit in the middle), but the classes overlap almost completely. Baking looks like a continuum, and the Food.com tags are fuzzy labels painted on it. Nothing here is clustered or compressed: the geometry you see is the geometry that was computed.

The Aid

Every recipe is a point on the simplex of its five parts. A partition groups those parts: two groups make a 1-D axis, three a triangle, four a tetrahedron. The divider bar above the plot lets you drag parts between compartments and watch the space fold. Search matches ingredients, baking families, recipe names, and ratio conditions (flour : fat = 2 : 1), highlighting where the query lives. Click a point for its ratio card — the closest simple ratio, the per-100 readout, and its nearest book archetype and class neighbourhood — and use the clipboard icon to copy the ratio as text.

The view is a link. The address bar records the partition, the selection, the search, and the display toggles, so a view can be bookmarked, shared or cited; the browser's Back and Forward buttons step through the views you visited.

The closest simple ratio

Each card snaps the recipe's five shares to the nearest simple whole-number ratio — the Ruhlman-style reading, e.g. 3 : 2 : 1 : 0 : 0 (flour : fat : sugar : liquid : egg). The search keeps only primitive ratios (no common divisor) and returns the simplest one whose own shares stay within 0.04 on every part — that is, no single part is more than four percentage points of the total mix away from where the round ratio would put it. "Simplest" means the smallest largest term, capped at 12. It is a reading aid, not part of the geometry — distances, centroids and classes all use the full shares.

For example, the ratio 3 : 2 : 1 : 0 : 0 is shares of 0.50 : 0.33 : 0.17 : 0 : 0. A recipe at 0.53 : 0.32 : 0.15 : 0 : 0 still snaps to it: the three non-zero parts differ by 0.03, 0.013 and 0.017, all under 0.04, even though none matches exactly. Because a ratio may sit that far off on any part, the Aid prefers round numbers over an exact but uglier match. About 2% of the plotted recipes land exactly on a small ratio and the rest are within tolerance; only 9 of 28,858 (0.03%) have none inside the tolerance and fall back to the closest ratio at the cap.

Where the data comes from

Three independent corpora meet at exactly one place: the numeric Food.com id at the end of each RecipeNLG link. The join is the only point of contact between the ingredients and the labels.

2.23 M recipes · ~2.3 GB

Food.com

267,782 recipes

7,793 foods · SR Legacy 2018-04

The final Standard Reference release. Per-100 g proximates decompose each ingredient head into structural parts.

Full vs structural

Two decomposition conventions were tested, and the choice between them is the project's main methodological decision.

  • Full — every ingredient's USDA composition is pooled into the parts, including add-ins (chocolate, nuts, fruit, cheese). A chocolate-chip cookie's chocolate enters as sugar and fat.
  • Structural — add-ins are zeroed: only structure-forming ingredients count (flours, sugars, fats, eggs, dairy, water, leaveners, salt, yeast), reproducing Ruhlman's clean ratio. One documented exception: water-dominant add-ins (applesauce, pumpkin, banana, zucchini, berries) contribute their water fraction to the liquid part; their remaining parts stay zeroed.

Describing the classes favors the add-in-excluding convention (structural separation −0.005 vs −0.018 full), so structural is what the pipeline ships. The full vector still exists internally — it picks each ingredient's primary part for the density lookup — but it never reaches the dataset.

Caveats

This is a vibe-coded project. The code is largely AI-generated, written with a language model over many sessions. It is carefully tested and reproducible, but treat the interpretations as a hobbyist's, not a paper's.

The preprocessing is approximate. Ruhlman's five parts are a deliberate simplification: ingredient lines are parsed heuristically, densities and USDA decomposition are best-effort, the hardest ingredients are dropped, non-baked sections are excluded, and the join to Food.com is imperfect. The geometry is only as clean as that pipeline.

Everything is plain Python and vanilla JavaScript, with no framework and no server.
